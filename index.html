We believe the most radical thing a magazine can do right now is slow down. To stand at the edge of a field, a fjord, a forest — and simply pay attention to what is still growing.

This winter we went looking for edges — the thin places where one world becomes another. Where moss becomes stone, where tide becomes sand, where a quiet person becomes, for an afternoon, a storyteller.

What we found was not wilderness, exactly, but something more intimate: a set of relationships still worth tending. These eleven stories are our attempt to hold them carefully.

The Spiral at the Edge of the World

Nobody knows who laid the first stone. The farmers say it was a bored shepherd; the guidebooks say it was a tourist; the children of the glen say, with total confidence, that it was there before any of us. Each year the spiral grows by a few more stones, carried up from the burn by strangers who will never meet.

I came for three days and stayed for eleven. The weather would not let me leave, and after a while I stopped asking it to. The light in this part of the world doesn’t fall so much as drift — it arrives sideways, green and gold, through a sky that changes its mind every quarter of an hour.

“The land here does not perform for you. It simply continues, and invites you to continue with it.”

On the fourth morning I met Eilidh, who has walked the same ridge every day for forty years. She doesn’t photograph it. She doesn’t write about it. “I just check it’s still there,” she told me, “and it always is. That’s the whole of my religion.”

On my last evening I carried a stone up from the water and set it at the outermost turn of the spiral. It was not a grand gesture. It was barely a gesture at all. But it is still there, I am told, and so — in some small way — am I.

Mara Okafor on learning to listen to rivers

The hydrologist and sound artist has spent a decade recording rivers from source to sea. We met her between field trips to talk about attention, patience and the music of moving water.

What does a river sound like when it’s healthy?

Complicated. A healthy river is never one note. You hear the gravel, the insects, the birds that follow the insects. Silence is usually the first sign that something has gone wrong.

And what has the work taught you?

That listening is a form of care. You cannot protect something you have never really heard.

“You cannot protect something you have never really heard.”
